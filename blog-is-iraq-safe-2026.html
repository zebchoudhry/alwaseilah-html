Is Iraq Safe
to Visit in 2026?

✍️ By Adam Jebrin 📅 July 2026 🕐 8 min read
Who wrote this and why it matters. Adam Jebrin has been running guided tours to Iraq since 2009 — through the US withdrawal, the rise and fall of ISIS, and the stabilisation of the regions he operates in. This is not a travel blogger's first impressions. It is the view from 17 years of making the same judgements every time a tour departs.

The short answer

Iraq is safe to visit in the regions most tourists want to visit. The pilgrimage cities of Najaf and Karbala receive millions of international visitors every year. Babylon is 40 minutes from Karbala. Iraqi Kurdistan — Erbil, Duhok, Sulaymaniyah — functions as a semi-autonomous region with its own security forces and a functioning tourism industry. Baghdad is a capital city of 8 million people living ordinary lives.

There are parts of Iraq that are not safe for tourists, and we do not go there. The western Anbar desert, areas along the Syrian border, and parts of Diyala province remain dangerous. We have never operated in those regions in 17 years and we never will.

The Iraq you see on the news and the Iraq your tour group visits are two different places.

What the FCDO advice actually means

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office advises against all but essential travel to most of Iraq. Many UK travellers read this as a blanket "do not go" and stop there. That is not what it means.

FCDO travel advice is a legal and insurance designation. It is issued conservatively because it covers the entire country under a single risk framework. The same advisory that covers Anbar province also covers Karbala — even though Karbala has received 20+ million pilgrims annually for years without a significant tourist safety incident.

Iraqi Kurdistan carries separate, less restrictive FCDO guidance, which reflects its genuinely different security situation.

The practical implication for UK travellers: standard travel insurance does not cover travel to Iraq. You need a specialist policy from a company like Campbell Irvine Direct or High Risk Voyager that explicitly covers Iraq. This is mandatory. We discuss this in every pre-booking call.

You should always read the current FCDO advice yourself at gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/iraq before deciding to travel.

Region by region: the honest picture

Southern Iraq — Najaf, Karbala, Basra, Nasiriyah

Southern Iraq has been stable for over a decade. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala are among the most visited religious sites on earth: in 2025, the Arbaeen pilgrimage alone brought 22 million people to Karbala. The security infrastructure around the shrines is extensive — permanent barriers, dedicated pilgrim police, and multiple checkpoints on entry routes.

The Islamic Pilgrimage Tour has run here every year since 2009 without a single security incident affecting a group member. The South Iraq Express covers Basra and the Mesopotamian Marshes — a UNESCO World Heritage landscape that feels entirely removed from any conflict narrative.

Baghdad

Baghdad is a functioning capital city. The Green Zone restrictions that once defined movement have significantly loosened. The Iraq National Museum — one of the world's great collections of Mesopotamian antiquity — is open to visitors. Mutanabbi Street, the historic booksellers' market, is busy on Fridays. The Abbasid Palace stands on the Tigris.

Baghdad requires sensible precautions: avoid large demonstrations, do not photograph security infrastructure, and move with a guide who has current local knowledge. With those in place, it is a remarkable city to visit.

Babylon and Central Iraq

Babylon — a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2019 — sits in Babil Governorate, about 85km south of Baghdad. The drive from Karbala takes around 40 minutes. Security along this route is good; this is part of the main southern pilgrimage corridor. In 2024, Babylon received over 5,000 foreign visitors, a number that is growing as awareness of the site increases.

Iraqi Kurdistan — Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, Duhok, Amadiyah

Iraqi Kurdistan is a semi-autonomous region with its own elected government, security forces (the Peshmerga), and currency. It has its own international airports (Erbil and Sulaymaniyah). Experienced independent travellers consistently describe Erbil as feeling safer than many European cities on a day-to-day basis.

Our North Iraq & Kurdistan Tour covers the ancient Erbil Citadel — continuously inhabited for 6,000 years and a UNESCO site — plus the mountain town of Amadiyah and the post-ISIS city of Mosul, where rebuilding is visibly underway.

Mosul

Mosul was liberated from ISIS in 2017. The old city is in active reconstruction: the Al-Nuri Mosque and its famous leaning minaret are being rebuilt, old city streets are reopening, and a small but genuine tourism scene has emerged. We have operated in Mosul since 2019 using local guide networks built over years of relationship. It is not a comfortable or easy destination — but it is a historically and humanly significant one.

Where we do not go

We do not operate in western Anbar province, the Syrian border regions, or areas of Diyala and Saladin provinces outside established tour routes. These areas carry genuine risk. We have never taken a group there and we never will. If you ask us about visiting them, we will tell you honestly that it is not something we can facilitate.

The 22 million who visited last year

In August 2025, 22 million pilgrims converged on Karbala for Arbaeen — the annual commemoration of the martyrdom of Imam Hussain. This is the world's largest annual peaceful gathering, larger than Hajj (approximately 2 million), and it has taken place every year without a catastrophic safety failure. Pilgrims walk hundreds of kilometres on foot from Najaf to Karbala along a route lined with free food, water and rest stations maintained by local communities.

This is not the picture of Iraq that makes international headlines. But it is the picture of Iraq that 22 million people have seen with their own eyes.

Practical safety for UK tourists

  • Travel insurance: Mandatory. Standard UK policies do not cover Iraq. You need a specialist policy explicitly covering the regions you visit. We recommend Campbell Irvine Direct or High Risk Voyager.
  • Dress code: Modest dress throughout. Women must cover hair and wear full-length clothing at religious sites. An abaya is required inside shrine complexes. Men should wear long trousers at all sites.
  • Photography: Never photograph checkpoints, bridges, security forces, government buildings, or embassies. At shrines, ask before photographing people. This is not unusual caution — it is a firm rule.
  • Season: Travel October–April. Iraqi summers are extreme: June–August temperatures regularly exceed 45°C in Baghdad and the south. This is not a time to visit.
  • Guide: A guide with genuine local knowledge is not optional. Access to many sites requires permits arranged in advance; checkpoints require local vouching; the language barrier is significant. The value of a guide who has done this 50+ times is not abstract.

The honest bottom line

Iraq is not for everyone. It requires preparation, specialist insurance, sensible behaviour, and a willingness to move through a country that is genuinely different from conventional tourist destinations. The rewards — ancient Mesopotamia, some of the world's most significant religious sites, extraordinary hospitality, and a landscape that shaped the entire human story — are proportional to that effort.

The question we are asked most often after a tour ends is: "Why did I wait so long?"
