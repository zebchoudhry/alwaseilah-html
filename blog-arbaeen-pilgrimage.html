What is Arbaeen?

22 million people walk to Karbala every year. It is bigger than Hajj, open to non-Muslims, and almost unknown in the West.

✍️ By Adam Jebrin 📅 July 2026 🕐 7 min read
Adam Jebrin has guided British pilgrims to Arbaeen since 2009. He was born in Iraq, grew up in the UK, and has walked the Karbala road himself. What follows is what he would tell you before you decide whether to go.

What Arbaeen is

Arbaeen (also spelled Arbain or Arba'een — Arabic for "forty") is the commemoration held forty days after Ashura, the day of the martyrdom of Imam Hussain ibn Ali at the Battle of Karbala in 680 CE. It falls on the 20th day of Safar in the Islamic lunar calendar.

In 2025, the Arbaeen pilgrimage drew an estimated 22 million participants to Karbala, southern Iraq — making it the world's largest annual peaceful gathering of people. For comparison, the Hajj — the most famous pilgrimage in Islam — draws approximately 1.8 to 2 million. Arbaeen is more than ten times larger, and almost entirely unknown to a Western audience.

The central act of Arbaeen is the Arbaeen Walk: a 78km foot pilgrimage from Najaf to the shrine of Imam Hussain in Karbala, completed over two to three days. Pilgrims walk through a corridor of moukibs — volunteer rest stations set up every few hundred metres by Iraqi families and communities — providing free food, tea, water, medical support, and accommodation to every walker, regardless of nationality or religion. The moukib system is one of the most extraordinary acts of collective hospitality on earth.

When is Arbaeen?

Arbaeen falls on the 20th of Safar in the Islamic lunar calendar. Because the Islamic calendar is lunar, the Gregorian date shifts by approximately 10–11 days each year.

  • Arbaeen 2026: approximately 13 August 2026
  • Arbaeen 2027: approximately 2 August 2027
  • Arbaeen 2028: approximately 22 July 2028

Note that August is peak summer heat in southern Iraq, with temperatures frequently exceeding 45°C during the day. Experienced pilgrims walk primarily at night during the summer Arbaeen cycle. Our tour includes timing advice and night-walk logistics built on years of experience.

The history behind it

On the 10th of Muharram 61 AH (October 10, 680 CE), Imam Hussain ibn Ali — grandson of the Prophet Muhammad — was killed with 72 of his companions at the Battle of Karbala after refusing to pledge allegiance to the Umayyad Caliph Yazid I. His death marks a pivotal moment in Islamic history: the event from which the Shia-Sunni split deepened, and which has shaped Shia identity and theology ever since.

The first Arbaeen pilgrimage was made 40 days after Karbala by Jabir ibn Abdullah al-Ansari, a companion of the Prophet, who walked to Karbala to pray over Imam Hussain's grave. This act of individual devotion became, over 1,400 years, the gathering of tens of millions.

During the Saddam Hussein era, Arbaeen was banned. Pilgrims were imprisoned or killed for gathering at Karbala. The pilgrimage resumed openly only after 2003. The scale of attendance since then — growing from hundreds of thousands to 22 million in two decades — reflects both the depth of devotion and the release of decades of suppression.

Can non-Muslims attend Arbaeen?

Yes. Arbaeen is open to people of all faiths. A significant proportion of pilgrims in recent years have been Christians, Yazidis, Sabeans, and non-religious visitors, particularly from Iraq, Iran, and Lebanon, where communities have close historical ties to the pilgrimage. International visitors from Europe, the Americas, and Southeast Asia attend as observers, journalists, academics, and spiritual seekers.

The moukib system makes no distinction: food, water, and rest are offered to everyone. You do not need to be Muslim to walk, to enter the outer areas of Karbala, or to observe the ceremonies.

Non-Muslims are not permitted inside the inner sanctums of the Imam Hussain Shrine or the Abbas Shrine. The shrine complex grounds are generally accessible; the inner chambers are reserved for Muslim pilgrims.

Modest dress is mandatory for everyone: women must cover their hair and wear full-length modest clothing; an abaya is recommended for comfort and respect. Men should wear long trousers.

What the walk is actually like

The Arbaeen Walk is 78km. Experienced walkers complete it in two days; those who take their time over three. The route runs from the Imam Ali Shrine in Najaf to the Imam Hussain Shrine in Karbala through the Iraqi desert.

Along the entire route, moukibs are spaced every few hundred metres. These are temporary structures — tents, covered canopies, permanent family compounds — maintained by Iraqi communities, Iranian groups, Lebanese organisations, and international community groups. They provide: hot food (rice, meat, lentils — free to all), water, tea, and cold drinks; rest areas and sleeping space; shoe cleaning and repair; medical tents with doctors and nurses; mobile phone charging points.

The atmosphere is unlike anything else. There is grief and remembrance — Arbaeen is a mourning occasion — but also extraordinary warmth, shared purpose, and human solidarity on a scale that is genuinely difficult to describe. Accounts from first-time visitors consistently emphasise being taken by surprise: they expected something more austere and found something more alive.

Arbaeen 2026 — what to expect

Arbaeen 2026 falls in mid-August. Summer Arbaeen is hot: we advise pilgrims to plan primarily for night walking, with rest during the hottest midday hours. The Iraqi government has significantly expanded the infrastructure along the Najaf-Karbala route in recent years: paved paths, additional lighting, expanded moukib zones, and a larger medical presence. The experience has become more organised without losing the spontaneous collective generosity that defines it.
